# Constructivist Call

A visual philosophy of agitation through pure form. Where words are the
last resort and geometry does most of the speaking. The work belongs to
the tradition that hung Rodchenko's diagonals on factory walls in 1925,
that filled Bauhaus presses with cyan and ink in 1928, and that still
makes someone stop on a quiet street in 2026 because a poster has
*claimed* them.

## The space and the form

Compositions are built like architecture, not arranged like graphics.
A single oversized disc anchors the field — its weight is felt before
it is seen. Diagonals slice the canvas at deliberate angles, never
ornamental, always functional, dividing the page into territories of
mass and air. The page is not a frame for content; it is a stage,
and every element earns its position through painstaking refinement.
The hand of a master printer — someone who has set type for forty
years and knows what one millimetre means — must be felt across
every quadrant.

## Colour and material

The palette is brutally restrained. Ink that approaches but never
reaches pure black. Deep imperial purple where Soviet poster artists
once used vermillion. A single sheet of bone-white paper, never
reflective. The result is a chromatic system reduced to three
voices, calibrated with master-level precision so that the absence
of further colour reads as confidence, not poverty. Every gradient,
every halo, every shadow has been laboured over until the surface
feels printed rather than rendered.

## Scale and rhythm

Typography arrives in two scales, never three. There is the SHOUT —
a single condensed word large enough that it cannot be politely
ignored, set so tightly that the letters touch but never collide —
and there is the whisper: small monospace marks, numbered, precise,
the sort of thing a typesetter would have hand-leaded in a press
shop. The middle register is forbidden. Either the form is
monumental or it is a footnote. This binary is the philosophy's
core discipline.

## Composition and balance

The composition obeys an asymmetric grid drawn before any element
is placed — twelve columns, generous outer margins, baselines that
hold across the entire surface. Diagonals are calculated, never
guessed. Numbers count up the side of the page like a manuscript
folio. Negative space is treated as raw material, not as the
leftover from arranging things; the empty rectangles are designed
with the same attention as the filled ones. A viewer should feel
the rigour even before they understand it.

## Visual hierarchy

Information lives where the eye lands, in the order the eye lands
on it. The disc first. The shout second. The numbered marginalia
third, fourth, fifth — read only by those who have already been
caught. There are no decorative flourishes. There are no
illustrations of what the words describe. The text and the geometry
are the same gesture; remove either and the other collapses. The
final artefact must look as though a single craftsman spent days at
a litho-stone, days at a drafting table, days at a typecase — and
the result is the artefact of that obsession, ready to be pinned to
a wall and read across a room.

## Final discipline

Every element on every page is the product of countless decisions
revisited and refined. Spacing has been measured against the
optical centre, not the geometric centre. The single shouted word
has been set, killed, reset, killed, reset until the kerning
disappears into the meaning. Nothing — not one rule, not one disc,
not one folio number — is incidental. The work must look as though
it could hang in a museum of graphic design, attributed to a master,
and the date and authorship would be forgiven for being uncertain.
